Birdhouse with Removable Roof

⏱ 3–4 hrs📏 6"W × 6"D × 9"H🪵 Cedar or pine💰 ~$15
Beginner +
A traditional gable-roof birdhouse with a hinged or lift-off roof for cleaning between seasons. Built from a single 1×6 cedar board — naturally rot-resistant, no finish needed. The 1-1/2" entry hole sized for chickadees, wrens, and nuthatches.

Tools

  • Handsaw or miter saw
  • Drill with 1-1/2" hole saw
  • Drill with 1/16" and 1/8" bits
  • Sandpaper (120, 220 grit)
  • Square and pencil
  • Tape measure
  • Hammer or driver

Materials

  • (1) 1×6 × 6' cedar board (rough or smooth)
  • (2) 1-1/2" galvanized hinges (optional, for hinged roof)
  • (1) small eye hook for hanging

Hardware

  • (20) #6 × 1-1/4" galvanized or stainless screws
  • Exterior wood glue (optional — Titebond III)

Cut List

Front / Back23/4" × 5-1/2" × 9"Front gets 1-1/2" hole, 6" up from bottom
Side23/4" × 4" × 8"Top edge cut at 30° for roof slope
Floor13/4" × 4" × 4"Drill (4) 1/4" drainage holes near corners
Roof, short13/4" × 4-1/2" × 7"

Assembly

  1. Cut all parts to size from your 1×6. Mark and cut the gable peaks on the front and back panels — measure 7" up from the bottom on each side, then bevel up to a centered peak at 9".
  2. On the front panel, drill the 1-1/2" entry hole 6" up from the bottom edge, centered. Sand the inside of the hole smooth.
  3. Drill (4) 1/4" drainage holes in the floor, one in each corner about 1/2" in from the edges.
  4. Drill (2) 1/8" ventilation holes near the top of each side panel.
  5. Pre-drill all screw holes with the 1/16" bit to prevent splitting — cedar splits easily near the edges.
  6. Attach the floor to the back panel, set 1/4" up from the bottom edge, with two screws.
  7. Attach both side panels to the back and floor (4 screws each side).
  8. Attach the front panel — secure to the floor and one side; leave the opposite side unscrewed if you want side-pivot access for cleaning.
  9. Lay the roof panels in place: the short panel butts against the long panel at the peak. Either screw down permanently or attach with hinges along the peak for a cleanable lid.
  10. Install an eye hook in the back panel about 1" down from the peak for hanging.

Finish

Leave cedar unfinished — it weathers to a beautiful silver-gray that birds prefer over painted surfaces. If you must finish, use a clear exterior penetrating oil only on the outside; never finish the interior or near the entry hole.

Tips & Variations

  • Adjust the entry hole diameter for your local birds: 1-1/8" for wrens, 1-1/2" for chickadees, 1-3/4" for bluebirds.
  • Skip the perch — perches help predators more than they help birds.
  • Score shallow horizontal grooves on the inside of the front panel below the entry hole; fledglings need them to climb out.

  • (1) 2×2 × 4' cedar (for corner posts)

Hardware

Assembly

  1. Cut all pieces to length. Cedar tears out easily — score your cut line first with a utility knife if using a handsaw.
  2. Sand all faces lightly with 120 grit. Knock down sharp edges with a few passes of a block plane or sandpaper.
  3. Stand two corner posts upright spaced 24" apart. Lay the bottom long slat across them, flush with the bottom of the posts. Pre-drill and drive two screws through the slat into each post.
  4. Add the middle and top long slats with a 1/4" gap between them — use a scrap of 1/4" plywood as a spacer.
  5. Repeat with the second pair of corner posts and the other long side.
  6. Stand the two assembled long sides upright, parallel, 8-1/2" apart. Screw the short slats across between them, again with 1/4" gaps.
  7. Drop the floor boards in from the top with a 1/2" gap between them. Screw down through the boards into the bottom slats.
  8. Attach rubber feet or cedar shims to the bottom of the corner posts to lift the planter off the ground.

Finish

None required. Cedar develops a silver patina in about a year outdoors. If you want to keep the warm orange color, apply a UV-blocking exterior oil like Penofin once a year.

Tips & Variations

  • Stretch this design longer — same construction works for 36" or 48" boxes; just add a fifth corner post mid-span if going past 36".
  • Line the inside with landscape fabric (not plastic) before filling with soil — keeps dirt from escaping the gaps without trapping moisture against the wood.
  • For a deeper box, scale the side slats from 3-1/2" to 5-1/2" and use 1×6 stock throughout.

Wall-Mounted Coat Rack

⏱ 2 hrs📏 30"L × 5"H × 4"D🪵 Maple or oak💰 ~$20
Beginner +
A clean, classic wall-mounted rack with five turned shaker pegs. Hangs from a French cleat for invisible mounting and easy leveling. A great early project — almost no joinery, but the result looks intentional and lasts decades.

Tools

  • Miter saw or circular saw
  • Drill with 1/2" Forstner bit (matching peg tenon)
  • Table saw or circular saw with rip guide (for cleat)
  • Sandpaper (120, 180, 220 grit)
  • Level and stud finder (for install)

Materials

  • (1) 1×5 × 36" hardwood (maple, oak, or walnut)
  • (1) 1×4 × 36" pine (for cleat — hidden)
  • (5) 3-1/2" shaker pegs with 1/2" tenons

Hardware

  • Titebond III wood glue
  • (4) #8 × 2-1/2" wood screws (for wall mount)
  • Finish of choice (oil, lacquer, or wax)

Cut List

PartQtyDimensionsNotes
Backboard13/4" × 4-1/2" × 30"Round or chamfer edges
Wall cleat13/4" × 3" × 28"Cut at 45° along length
Rack cleat13/4" × 3" × 28"Mating 45° cut
Shaker peg53-1/2" L × 1/2" tenonStore-bought

Assembly

  1. Cut the backboard to 30". Sand all faces and edges through 220.
  2. Mark peg locations: starting from the left, measure 3", 9", 15", 21", and 27" — five marks centered vertically on the backboard.
  3. Drill 1/2" Forstner-bit holes at each peg location, 1/2" deep. Test-fit a peg; the tenon should slide in with light hand pressure.
  4. Apply a small dab of glue in each hole, insert pegs, and tap home with a rubber mallet. Wipe excess glue immediately.
  5. Rip the French cleat: take your 1×4 pine, set the table saw to 45°, and rip it down the middle of the long edge. You now have two matching beveled pieces.
  6. Glue and screw one cleat half to the back of the rack, bevel facing up and toward the wall, top edge flush with the top of the backboard.
  7. To install: locate wall studs, screw the second cleat to the wall (bevel facing down and toward the wall) with #8 × 2-1/2" screws into studs. Hang the rack onto the cleat.

Finish

For oak or maple, two coats of penetrating oil (Tried & True, Osmo Polyx) give a soft satin look. For a darker piece, walnut takes a wiped-on oil-based stain beautifully — finish with paste wax or thin lacquer.

Tips & Variations

  • Round the corners of the backboard with a 1/2" roundover bit, or chamfer them at 45° for a more shaker look.
  • Substitute cast-iron hooks for the shaker pegs to lean industrial / farmhouse.
  • Add a top shelf — glue a 4"-deep ledge to the top edge of the backboard for hats and gloves.

End-Grain Cutting Board

⏱ 4–5 hrs (over 2 days)📏 14" × 10" × 1-1/4"🪵 Maple + walnut💰 ~$30
Beginner +
A striped end-grain cutting board that's gentle on knife edges and gorgeous on the counter. Two glue-ups: first a long-grain striped panel, then crosscut into strips and reassembled end-grain-up. The finished board self-heals from knife marks and lasts a lifetime.

Tools

  • Table saw with crosscut sled
  • Planer or hand plane
  • Random-orbit sander
  • (8+) bar clamps
  • Sandpaper: 80, 120, 180, 220, 320 grit
  • Painter's tape (for clamping)

Materials

  • (1) 4/4 hard maple, 36" × 6" (rough or surfaced)
  • (1) 4/4 walnut, 36" × 4" (rough or surfaced)

Hardware

  • Titebond III waterproof glue (food-safe when cured)
  • Mineral oil + beeswax board cream

Cut List

PartQtyDimensionsNotes
Maple strip53/4" × 1-1/2" × 16"For first glue-up
Walnut strip43/4" × 1-1/2" × 16"For first glue-up
Cross-cut slices9cut at 1-1/2" from glued panelAfter first glue-up dries

Assembly

  1. Mill all stock to a uniform 3/4" thickness. Joint one edge of every strip straight — your final glue line depends on this.
  2. Rip strips to 1-1/2" wide and crosscut to 16" long. You should have 5 maple and 4 walnut strips.
  3. First glue-up: alternate maple/walnut/maple/walnut, glue and clamp into a striped panel. Use cauls top and bottom to keep it flat. Let dry 24 hours.
  4. Plane the panel flat on both faces, removing glue squeeze-out. Final thickness around 11/16".
  5. Crosscut the panel into 9 strips at 1-1/2" wide using a crosscut sled — these are now end-grain-up strips.
  6. Flip every other strip end-for-end so the maple/walnut pattern shifts (creates a checkerboard). Or align them all the same way for clean stripes — your call.
  7. Second glue-up: glue and clamp the 9 strips together with cauls and plenty of clamps. End-grain glue-ups need slightly more pressure than long-grain. Let dry 24 hours.
  8. Flatten the board: a planer is risky on end grain (tearout, kickback). Use a router sled with a flattening bit, or take it slow with a belt sander, then random-orbit through 80, 120, 180, 220, 320.
  9. Round all four edges with a 1/4" roundover bit or sand by hand. Pay extra attention to corners.
  10. Wet the entire board with water to raise the grain. Let dry, then sand once more at 320.
  11. Apply mineral oil generously — flood the surface, let soak 20 minutes, wipe off excess. Repeat over 3 days. Finish with a coat of board cream (mineral oil + beeswax).

Finish

Mineral oil only — never linseed, tung, or any drying oil that turns rancid. Re-oil monthly for the first six months, then whenever the board "looks thirsty." Hand-wash only; never put a wood board in the dishwasher.

Tips & Variations

  • For a 3D effect, do a third glue-up: cut your end-grain panel into strips again, shift, and reglue — gives you a brick-pattern look.
  • Add juice grooves with a 1/2" core-box bit and a router edge guide before final sanding.
  • Cherry or sapele make great alternatives to walnut. Avoid open-pore woods like red oak — they trap food.

Floating Shelf with Hidden Bracket

⏱ 2–3 hrs📏 24"L × 8"D × 1-3/4"H🪵 Walnut or oak💰 ~$25
Beginner +
A solid-wood floating shelf with no visible brackets. The shelf is hollow on the back, sliding over a hidden steel rod bracket that's lag-screwed into wall studs. Looks impossibly clean — like the wood is growing out of the wall.

Tools

  • Table saw or circular saw
  • Drill with 1/2" long bit (12"+)
  • Stud finder, level
  • Sandpaper (120, 180, 220 grit)
  • Hammer (for gentle tap-fit)

Materials

  • (1) 8/4 walnut or oak slab, 24" × 8" (or glue up two 4/4 boards)
  • (1) heavy-duty floating shelf bracket kit (Ekena, Federal Brace, etc.) — or fabricate from 1/2" steel rod

Hardware

  • (2) 3/8" × 4" lag screws (or supplied with kit)
  • Wood glue (if gluing up the slab)
  • Finish (oil, lacquer, or paste wax)

Cut List

PartQtyDimensionsNotes
Shelf body11-3/4" × 8" × 24"From 8/4 stock or glued-up 4/4

Assembly

  1. If gluing up the slab, joint two 4/4 boards' inner edges and glue face-to-face (or edge-to-edge for a wider piece). Clamp flat. Plane to 1-3/4" final thickness once dry.
  2. Cut the slab to 24" × 8" final dimensions. Sand through 220 grit. Soften the visible edges with a 1/8" roundover or chamfer.
  3. Locate two wall studs where the shelf will hang. Mark them on the wall.
  4. Hold the bracket up to the wall, level, with the rods centered between the studs (they should fall on the studs). Mark and drill pilot holes for the lag screws.
  5. On the back edge of the shelf, transfer the rod positions exactly. Use the bracket itself to mark — mount the bracket loosely, hold the shelf against it, and mark where the rods touch the wood.
  6. Drill matching 1/2" holes into the back edge of the shelf, 6" deep. Drill straight and true — a drill press is ideal but a steady hand and a drilling guide work fine.
  7. Test-fit the shelf onto the bracket without screwing the bracket to the wall. Adjust hole depth or position as needed.
  8. Mount the bracket to the studs with lag screws, level it, then slide the shelf onto the rods. It should slide on snug; tap gently with a rubber mallet if needed.

Finish

Walnut looks stunning with two coats of pure tung oil or Osmo Polyx. For oak, use a darker finish like Watco Danish Oil to bring out the grain. Apply finish before final installation — much easier than working around the wall.

Tips & Variations

  • For shelves longer than 36", upgrade to a 3-rod bracket and 5/8" rods minimum.
  • Live-edge slabs work beautifully here — keep the natural edge as the front, square the back to fit the bracket.
  • The hidden cavity can hold an LED strip — add a notch on the bottom edge for the wire and you've got built-in accent lighting.

Mitered Picture Frame with Splines

⏱ 3 hrs📏 Holds 11×14 print🪵 Cherry or walnut💰 ~$15
Beginner +
A clean, simple frame with mitered corners reinforced by contrasting wood splines — both decorative and structural. Sized for an 11×14 print or photo with a 2" mat. Once you've cut a few miters, you can scale this for any photo size.

Tools

  • Miter saw (good blade — 80T+) or table saw with miter sled
  • Router with 3/8" rabbeting bit
  • Band saw or table saw (for spline slots)
  • Spline jig (shop-built or store-bought)
  • Strap clamp or band clamp
  • Sandpaper (120, 180, 220)

Materials

  • (1) 1×3 × 8' cherry or walnut
  • (1) scrap of contrasting wood (maple or holly) for splines
  • 11×14 glass and backing board

Hardware

  • Titebond III glue
  • (4) frame clips or sawtooth hanger
  • Finish — wiping varnish or oil

Cut List

PartQtyDimensionsNotes
Frame, long23/4" × 2" × ~17" (mitered)Inside length 14-3/4"
Frame, short23/4" × 2" × ~14" (mitered)Inside length 11-3/4"
Spline41/8" × 1/2" × 3/4"Cut from contrasting scrap

Assembly

  1. Mill the frame stock to 3/4" × 2". Sand the front face through 180 — easier now than after assembly.
  2. Set up your router with a 3/8" rabbeting bit and rabbet the back inside edge of the stock 3/8" deep × 3/8" wide. This holds the glass, art, and backing.
  3. Test your miter saw with scrap. Cut and fit a four-corner test frame; adjust the angle until joints close perfectly with no gaps.
  4. Cut the long frame pieces with the inside edge measuring 14-3/4" (gives 14" + 3/8" rabbet on each side).
  5. Cut the short frame pieces with the inside edge measuring 11-3/4".
  6. Dry-fit the four pieces into a rectangle. Wrap a strap clamp around all four to test fit.
  7. Glue the miters: apply glue to all four corners, assemble in the strap clamp, and tighten evenly. Wipe squeeze-out. Let dry 1 hour minimum, ideally overnight.
  8. Once dry, set the frame in your spline jig with one corner pointing up. Cut a 1/8"-wide slot through the corner about 1/2" deep. Repeat all four corners.
  9. Cut splines from contrasting scrap — slightly oversize. Glue into slots, let dry, then trim flush with a flush-cut saw and sand.
  10. Final sand the frame through 220. Finish with two coats of wiping varnish or Danish oil.
  11. Drop in glass, art, and backing board. Secure with frame points or push pins from the back. Attach a sawtooth hanger or frame wire.

Finish

For cherry, finish before installing the print to avoid getting oil on the mat. Two coats of wipe-on poly with a light scuff between is foolproof. Walnut takes oil best — Tried & True original blend gives a warm, dark glow.

Tips & Variations

  • Scale the math: inside opening = print size + 2× rabbet width. So an 8×10 print needs 8-3/4" × 10-3/4" inside opening.
  • Splines look best in pairs — try cutting two parallel slots per corner for a doubled look.
  • For deeper "shadow box" frames, increase rabbet depth to 3/4" and use 1×4 stock instead.

Beginner +
A sturdy two-step stool that handles real weight — sized so a child can reach the bathroom sink or you can grab the top shelf. Joined with simple dadoes (no fancy joinery) and reinforced with screws. Will outlast plastic any day.

Tools

  • Table saw with dado stack (or router with straight bit)
  • Miter saw or circular saw
  • Drill, countersink bit
  • (4) bar clamps
  • Sandpaper (120, 180, 220)

Materials

  • (1) 1×12 × 6' clear pine or poplar
  • (1) 1×3 × 3' for back rail

Hardware

  • Titebond II glue
  • (12) #8 × 1-5/8" wood screws
  • Self-adhesive non-slip pads (top tread)
  • Finish — paint or polyurethane

Cut List

PartQtyDimensionsNotes
Side23/4" × 11-1/4" × 18"Cut step profile after dadoes
Top tread13/4" × 10" × 16"
Bottom tread13/4" × 8" × 16"
Back rail13/4" × 2-1/2" × 16"Adds rigidity

Assembly

  1. Cut both side panels to 11-1/4" × 18". Stack them and clamp together so any layout you do happens identically on both.
  2. Mark dado locations on one side panel: bottom tread sits at 9" up from the floor; top tread sits at 18" from the floor (flush with the top). Each dado is 3/4" wide × 1/4" deep.
  3. Cut both dadoes through both side panels at once on the table saw with a dado stack, or rout them with a straight bit and edge guide.
  4. Now lay out the step profile on one side: front edge of top tread is 8" from the back; the side angles forward from there to the bottom front corner. Cut both side panels to this profile (jigsaw or band saw).

  7. Glue and clamp the treads into the side dadoes. Reinforce with three screws per joint, driven from the outside through the side panel into the tread end grain. Countersink them for a flush look.
  8. Glue and screw the back rail across the back top edge — connects the two sides at the rear and prevents racking.
  9. Let dry, then sand all surfaces through 220 grit.
  10. Add self-adhesive non-slip pads to the top tread surface (especially if it'll be wet — bathroom or kitchen use).
  11. Finish with two coats of latex paint (kid-friendly) or polyurethane to show the wood grain.

Finish

If for a child, paint with low-VOC latex in a fun color and add their name. For adult use, two coats of satin polyurethane keeps the wood look while standing up to wet shoes.

Tips & Variations

  • Add cutout handles in the side panels — a 4" × 1-1/4" oval cut 2" below the top makes the stool easier to carry.
  • For a sturdier "real" stool, upgrade to red oak and use 5/8" dadoes plus glue blocks underneath each tread.
  • Add rubber feet or felt pads on the bottom — protects floors and quiets the stool when moved.

Chess & Checkers Board

Beginner +
A classic 8×8 inlaid board built using the strip-glue-and-crosscut method — same technique as the end-grain cutting board, scaled to 2" squares. Trimmed with a contrasting border. Heirloom-quality, and a great workout for table-saw accuracy.

Tools

  • Table saw with crosscut sled (essential)
  • Planer or hand plane
  • Bar clamps (8+)
  • Random-orbit sander
  • Sandpaper through 320 grit

Materials

  • (1) 4/4 hard maple, 36" × 5"
  • (1) 4/4 walnut, 36" × 5"
  • (1) walnut border stock, 6' of 3/4" × 1-1/2"

Hardware

  • Titebond III glue
  • Wipe-on polyurethane or shellac

Cut List

PartQtyDimensionsNotes
Maple strip43/4" × 2" × 17"For first glue-up
Walnut strip43/4" × 2" × 17"For first glue-up
Crosscut slices8cut at 2" wideReassemble alternating
Border, long23/4" × 1-1/2" × 17"Mitered
Border, short23/4" × 1-1/2" × 17"Mitered

Assembly

  1. Mill maple and walnut to a uniform 3/4" thickness. Joint one edge of every strip straight.
  2. Rip 8 strips total (4 maple, 4 walnut) at exactly 2" wide × 17" long.
  3. First glue-up: alternate maple/walnut/maple/walnut into a 16"-wide panel. Use cauls top and bottom; clamp evenly. Dry 24 hours.
  4. Plane both faces flat, removing glue squeeze-out. Final thickness around 11/16".
  5. Crosscut the panel into 8 strips at 2" wide using a crosscut sled. Mark the orientation of each strip before cutting so you don't lose track.

  9. Cut walnut border stock with mitered ends to fit snugly around the perimeter. Glue and clamp around the board.
  10. Sand the entire piece flat with a random-orbit sander, working through 80, 120, 180, 220, 320.
  11. Apply 3 thin coats of wipe-on poly or shellac, scuffing between coats with 320 grit. Final coat unsanded.

Finish

Wipe-on satin poly gives a hard, durable surface that pieces slide on smoothly. For a more traditional look, three coats of dewaxed shellac (Zinsser SealCoat) followed by paste wax — feels silky and looks period-correct.

Tips & Variations

  • Add felt pads to the bottom corners — protects tabletops and lets the board sit dead-flat.
  • Inlay rank/file letters and numbers (a/b/c, 1/2/3) along the border with a sign stencil and a small router.
  • Want a thicker, weightier board? Glue a 1/2" plywood backer to the bottom and trim flush.

Three-Shelf Wall Bookshelf

Assembly (overview — full build is documented over 6 chapters in the Maloof book)

  1. Build bending forms first: one for rockers (long, sweeping curve about 60" radius), one for the crest rail (gentler arc to fit the back).
  2. Resaw walnut for laminations to 3/8" thick × 2-1/2" wide × 42" long. Each rocker = 5 strips. Glue and clamp on the form for 24 hours minimum.
  3. Glue up the seat blank from 4–5 thick walnut boards into a 22" × 22" panel about 2" thick. Plane flat. Cut to a roughly horseshoe-shaped outline.
  4. Hollow the seat: clamp the seat to your bench upside-down. Use an angle grinder with a sanding disc, then progressively finer abrasives, to scoop out a comfortable seat shape — 2" deep at the deepest point, sloping up to flat at the front and rear.
  5. Shape the seat's outer profile: bandsaw and rasp the perimeter into the iconic Maloof "wedge" silhouette — taller at the back, sloping forward to a thin lip.
  6. Cut the four legs from 8/4 walnut. Front legs are short and slightly curved; back legs are 36" long with a graceful S-curve. Bandsaw the rough shape; refine with spokeshave.
  7. Now the famous Maloof joint: where each leg meets the seat, you create a flush, blended joint with no visible mortise. Cut a 5° "back-cut" angle on the top of each leg. Drill two #14 screw holes through the leg into the seat at angles. Cut matching shoulders into the seat where the leg lands.
  8. Test-fit each leg-to-seat joint dry. Adjust shoulder angles and cuts until everything seats perfectly with no gaps. The screws will pull the joint together.
  9. Drive screws to assemble each leg-to-seat joint. Plug the screw holes with walnut plugs, sanded flush.
  10. Now the visual signature work: with rasps, spokeshaves, and sandpaper, blend each leg into the seat as a continuous flowing curve. No square corners anywhere — every transition is a rounded sweep. This is where the "Maloof look" lives.
  11. Drill 7 holes in the back of the seat for spindle bottoms (5/8" deep). Shape spindles on a lathe or with a spokeshave — tapered slightly toward the top.
  12. Drill matching holes in the underside of the laminated crest rail. Glue spindles between seat and crest, with a slight forward bow built in (gives the back its curve).
  13. Shape and attach the headrest at the top of the crest rail; sculpt it into a continuous curve with the crest.
  14. Shape the arms; attach to the front leg tops and the back legs with hidden 1/2" dowels.
  15. Attach the rockers to the bottom of the legs with 1/2" dowels and glue. Sculpt the leg-to-rocker joint into a smooth blend.
  16. Sand the ENTIRE chair through 220 — every blended curve, every sculpted surface. Then sand again at 320. Then 400. Then 600. Maloof said the polishing was 80% of the work.
  17. Finish with Maloof's recipe: equal parts boiled linseed oil, pure tung oil, and varnish, wiped on. After cure, paste wax. Apply 3+ coats over a month for the deep glow.

Finish

The Sam Maloof Poly/Oil and Maloof Oil/Wax (sold under his name by Rockler) replicate his original recipe. Apply liberally with a cloth, let soak in for 30 min, wipe off everything excess, repeat over 3 coats. The walnut will glow.

Tips & Variations

  • Read Sam Maloof's "Sam Maloof, Woodworker" cover-to-cover before starting. Charles Brock's "The Maloof Inspired Rocker" video and book series teach the joint and finishing process in depth.
  • The leg-to-seat joint is the make-or-break detail. Practice on scrap walnut first — get the angles dialed before cutting the real legs.
  • Don't rush the sanding. Maloof's chairs feel like they were dipped in glass because they were sanded, oiled, sanded again, oiled, for a month.
